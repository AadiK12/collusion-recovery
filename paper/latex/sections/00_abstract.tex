AI-agent workflows increasingly ask models to review one another, but restoring
an agent's access to evidence may not restore independent verification if its
earlier interaction history favored approval without checking. We specify a
controlled recovery experiment with deterministic ground truth. Pairs of agents
first complete repeated reciprocal reviews under either complete or constrained
peer-evidence access. We then restore complete evidence and compare six matched
evaluation branches that vary prior history, an explicit corrective instruction,
and the incremental addition of visible-history reset. An action-matched
scripted-peer diagnostic separately tests sensitivity to an approving peer and
to a direct reciprocal-approval request while holding authoritative evidence
fixed. The primary outcome is false approval of objectively faulty packets;
correct-packet approval and balanced accuracy prevent indiscriminate rejection
from appearing protective. Inference is paired at the independent-block level,
preserves malformed or missing outcomes through all-scheduled bounds, and keeps
exploratory pilot data physically separate from fresh confirmation data. The
experiment distinguishes verification failure, history-associated carryover,
evaluation-time peer dependence, and a narrow confirmation-only operational
collusion criterion. As of this pre-results draft, no hypothesis-bearing empirical
run has occurred; numerical findings and the final evidence-calibrated implication
will be inserted only after the staged release, integrity, and claim checks pass.
