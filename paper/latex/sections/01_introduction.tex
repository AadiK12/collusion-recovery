\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Consider an AI agent reviewing a peer's claimed result. The protocol requires it
to verify the claim against source records, but the workflow initially withholds
those records. Every formation claim is correct, and both agents receive positive
feedback when their verdicts match the task outcome. Repeated approval can
therefore become locally successful even though it violates the evidence rule.
When the missing records later become available and a faulty claim appears, will
the agent resume independent verification, or will the earlier interaction still
shape its decision?

This is a recovery question, not merely an access-control question. Restoring
evidence makes correct verification possible, but it does not directly retract a
learned shortcut, warn against reciprocal approval, or remove the visible history
in which approval succeeded. Operationally, these are different interventions:
\emph{evidence repair} changes the information available now; \emph{explicit
correction} changes the current instruction; and \emph{history reset} changes the
visible state carried into the decision. If access repair alone leaves a harmful
residual gap, a system designer needs to know which additional intervention
changes behavior. If the residual gap is small, the absolute error rate and the
cost to correct approvals still determine whether the system has recovered
usefully.

Long-horizon reciprocal verification can produce sustained joint protocol
violations, and controlled peer interventions indicate that peer behavior can
matter \citep{shi2026emergent}. Separate work shows that agents adjust costly
verification after reliable or unreliable partners \citep{chen2026trust}, that
incorrect upstream messages can override an otherwise correct answer
\citep{gong2026upstream}, and that prior audit--repair context can shift a
verifier's decision threshold even when the present task is unchanged
\citep{mazaheri2026audit}. These findings motivate the risk but do not answer the
specific operator-facing question here: after full authoritative evidence is
restored, how much does verification behavior recover, and what does correction
or visible-history reset add?

We study that question in a deterministic record-selection audit. Two model
agents first complete ten reciprocal reviews under either full peer-evidence
access or a constrained regime in which the evidence required by the stated rule
is unavailable. We then clone immutable history endpoints into six matched
evaluation branches. Every branch sees newly generated packets with known truth;
the branches vary prior history, present evidence, corrective instruction, and
visible-history reset. A separate scripted-peer diagnostic holds the current task
evidence fixed while varying the peer's observable approval and whether its
message explicitly requests approval in return. This lets us distinguish an
aggregate history effect from current peer-message sensitivity.

The experiment deliberately separates four levels of evidence. A
\emph{verification failure} is approval of a faulty packet despite complete
current evidence. A \emph{post-restoration carryover effect} is a matched
difference under the same repaired environment between constrained- and
full-evidence histories. \emph{Evaluation-time peer dependence} requires an
intervention on current peer policy with authoritative evidence fixed. Finally,
the paper reserves a narrow, confirmation-only \emph{configuration-level
operational collusion} label for the conjunction of sustained joint protocol
violations and a sufficiently large action-matched reciprocal-request effect in
one immutable confirmation lineage. None of these measurements establishes
private intent, secrecy, weight change, or real-world conspiracy.

Our intended contributions are:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item an auditable recovery experiment whose primary failure is objectively
  faulty approval rather than agreement or self-reported trust;
  \item matched estimates for evidence restoration, explicit correction, and the
  incremental correction-plus-history-reset package;
  \item an action-matched peer intervention that separates the effect of an
  approving peer from the additional effect of a reciprocal-approval request;
  \item safety--usefulness reporting that pairs false approval with correct-packet
  approval so blanket rejection cannot masquerade as repair; and
  \item a reproducible, fail-closed workflow with deterministic truth, immutable
  branch lineage, all-scheduled missingness bounds, and physically separate pilot
  and confirmation inference paths.
\end{enumerate}

A separately versioned secondary study, \texttt{formation-peer-v1}, assigns an
always-approving or always-rejecting scripted peer during the constrained history
itself. It tests the total contribution of assigned peer policy through the
resulting feedback and transcript pathway. We specify it in Methods because its
offline design is complete, but it has no live release and contributes no
empirical claim unless run under its own prospective authorization and split.
The main recovery question remains primary regardless of that study's status.
